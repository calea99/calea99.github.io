About

I wanted to write for the internet. It turned out I wanted SEO.

I started out planning to be a novelist. Then the internet happened, and I wanted to write for it instead - even though nobody quite knew what that looked like yet. I went to grad school to learn more about this new communication technology and what it might mean for me.

My first job was at Consumers Digest Online, the magazine's original website. I collected product information for our database and wrote category descriptions and link teasers ("Jack's fax is lax. Jean's keen for a new machine."). Then the dot-com bubble burst.

Over the next several years, I learned the web from a few more angles. At Nando Media, later McClatchy Interactive, I prioritized and published wire news for nandotimes.com and around 30 affiliate sites, and was promoted to senior producer. At Conjecture, I wrote and edited for wiseGEEK, then hired and managed editors and ran projects. That's where I learned to write for search, where keywords and rankings drove the business. At Dex Media, I wrote ad copy and business descriptions for small businesses, and worked with clients to improve their performance.

Then I joined ROI Revolution as an SEO copywriter and quickly fell in love with SEO. This was what I'd meant all along by "writing for the internet": copy that ranks and that people actually want to read. Over the years, I added technical SEO to my toolkit, was promoted to the highest individual-contributor level, and then to Team Lead, leading a small team of technical analysts and content writers.

What I'm doing now

Search doesn't end at the results page anymore. People get answers from AI Overviews, ChatGPT, and Gemini, and those answers still depend on what good SEO always has: content worth citing, and pages machines can actually read.

So I build. I've worked with AI to create more than 20 tools for my team - skills that produce weekly competitive intelligence reports, research keywords, map content, and forecast traffic, plus a side-by-side view of how a person sees a page versus how an AI crawler sees it. I don't hand-write the code. I direct AI to build what I need, test it against real work, and keep improving it.

Two of my newest tools look at how AI sees a brand. An AI visibility tracker runs the non-branded questions customers actually ask through Google AI Mode, AI Overviews, ChatGPT, and Perplexity, and records how often the brand is named or cited compared with its competitors. A brand entity analysis, now in beta, builds on that. It checks how search engines and AI assistants understand who a brand is, what it offers, and how it fits its industry. It compares sources like Google's Knowledge Graph, Wikidata, and business listings against a confirmed facts sheet, and reviews key pages for clarity, evidence, and structured data. The result is accurate, consistent information for AI tools to work from, and a baseline to measure progress against - not a promise of placement in AI answers.
